## The Maya Drank Cacao, Not Coffee

The ancient Maya never tasted coffee. Coffee is native to the highlands of Ethiopia, and it didn't reach the Americas until Spanish and Dutch colonists brought it in the 1700s, thousands of years after the Maya had built and abandoned their great cities.

What the Maya drank was cacao. They cultivated it, traded it as currency, offered it to their gods, and consumed it as a bitter, frothy, chili-spiced drink called *xocolatl*. For over 3,000 years, cacao was the most valued crop in the Mayan world.

So when someone searches for "Mayan coffee," the honest answer is: there was no such thing.

## Copán Valley: Cacao Territory Turned Coffee Country

The highlands of western Honduras, the Copán Valley, the Sierra de Celaque, the slopes above the Lenca and Ch'orti' Maya territories, were the eastern edge of the ancient Mayan world. The [ruins of Copán](/blog/copan-honduras-coffee/), a UNESCO World Heritage Site, sit in the same valley where coffee now grows at 1,300 to 1,700 meters above sea level.

The volcanic soil that made Copán's cacao exceptional is the same soil that produces specialty-grade arabica today. The altitude that gave Mayan farmers cool nights and misty mornings gives modern coffee cherries the slow maturation that develops complex sugars and acids.

Coffee replaced cacao in these mountains, but the land didn't change. The families farming it, in many cases, didn't change either. The descendants of Maya communities in western Honduras and eastern Guatemala are the people growing, harvesting, and processing the coffee that ships from this region.

When we call our coffee "Mayan Origin," we're not inventing a marketing story. We're naming the place.

## Altitude, Volcanic Soil, and Shade Canopy

The regions of Honduras and Guatemala that overlap with ancient Mayan territory share a set of growing conditions that produce distinctive coffee:

**Altitude.** The Mayan highlands sit between 1,000 and 1,800 meters above sea level. Coffee grown above 1,200 meters is classified as Strictly High Grown (SHG), the highest quality grade. Higher altitude means cooler temperatures, slower cherry development, and denser beans with more concentrated flavor.

**Volcanic soil.** The mountains of western Honduras and highland Guatemala sit on volcanic geology. This soil is rich in minerals (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium) that coffee plants absorb and convert into the organic compounds that become flavor in the cup.

**Shade canopy.** Mayan farmers grew cacao under forest canopy. Modern coffee in these same regions is often shade-grown under native trees, a practice that slows ripening, reduces the need for chemical inputs, and supports biodiversity. It's the same agricultural logic the Maya used, applied to a different crop.

**Distinct seasons.** The wet season (May through October) feeds the plants. The dry season (November through March) triggers flowering and allows for clean, consistent drying of harvested cherries. This seasonal rhythm is what gives Central American coffees their clean, balanced profiles.

The result: coffee with chocolate and caramel sweetness, balanced acidity, stone fruit and citrus notes at higher elevations, and a clean finish.

## How Coffee Replaced Cacao in Honduras

The transition happened over about 200 years.

Cacao remained the dominant crop in Central America through the Spanish colonial period. But disease, changing trade routes, and competition from West African cacao plantations gradually reduced its profitability in Honduras and Guatemala.

Coffee arrived in Honduras in the late 1700s and expanded slowly through the 1800s. By the early 1900s, coffee had overtaken cacao as the region's primary export crop. The western highlands — Copán, Ocotepeque, Lempira, Santa Bárbara — became Honduras's most productive coffee zones.

Today [Honduras is the largest coffee producer in Central America](/blog/honduras-coffee/) and the sixth largest in the world, exporting roughly 7 to 8 million 60-kilogram bags per year. Much of the best coffee comes from the same western highlands that were once the heart of Mayan civilization.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Did the Maya drink coffee?**
No. Coffee is native to Ethiopia and was not introduced to the Americas until the 1700s. The Maya drank cacao, which they cultivated for over 3,000 years. The regions where the Maya grew cacao — the highlands of Honduras and Guatemala — are now major coffee-producing areas.

**What is Mayan coffee?**
Mayan coffee refers to coffee grown in regions that were part of ancient Mayan territory, particularly the highlands of Honduras, Guatemala, and southern Mexico. The volcanic soil, altitude, and climate that supported Mayan cacao cultivation now produce exceptional arabica coffee.

**Where does Mayan coffee come from?**
Most coffee from Mayan territory comes from Honduras and Guatemala — countries whose western highlands were the heart of ancient Mayan civilization. The Copán region of Honduras and the Huehuetenango region of Guatemala are two of the most important growing areas.

**Is Mayan coffee good?**
Coffee from the Mayan highlands of Honduras and Guatemala is among the best in the world. High altitude (1,300–1,800 meters), volcanic soil, and distinct wet/dry seasons produce dense, complex beans with notes of chocolate, stone fruit, and citrus. Several Honduran coffees from these regions have won the Cup of Excellence.

**What did the ancient Maya drink instead of coffee?**
The Maya drank cacao (*xocolatl*), atole (a warm corn-based drink), chicha (fermented corn beer), balché (fermented honey and bark), and fresh fruit drinks. Cacao was the most valued — it served as currency, ceremonial offering, and elite beverage.
