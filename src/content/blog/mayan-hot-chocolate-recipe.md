## The Drink That Came Before Chocolate

Before chocolate bars, before hot cocoa packets, before Swiss Miss: there was *xocolātl*.

The ancient Maya were the first civilization to consume cacao as a drink, and what they made would be almost unrecognizable to anyone who grew up on Swiss hot chocolate. No sugar. No milk. No cream. The original Mayan hot chocolate was bitter, frothy, spiced, and sometimes cold, depending on the ceremony.

It was not a dessert drink. It was a sacred one.

## A Brief History of Mayan Hot Chocolate

Cacao (*Theobroma cacao*, meaning "food of the gods" in Greek) originated in Mesoamerica. The Maya were cultivating and consuming cacao at least 3,500 years ago. There is archaeological evidence of cacao use at sites in Honduras dating to 1400 BCE.

The highland valleys of Copán, Honduras, where we source our private label coffees, were ancient cacao-growing territory. Cacao was so valued by the Maya that it served as currency. Merchants traded it across Mesoamerica from the Gulf Coast to the Pacific. Rulers stored it in treasuries.

The drink itself was prepared by grinding roasted cacao beans into a paste, mixing with water and spices, and creating froth by pouring the liquid from one vessel to another from a height. The froth was considered the most valuable part of the drink. The more froth, the better.

Mayan chocolate drinks were consumed:
- In royal courts and elite ceremonies
- Before warfare (for warriors seeking strength)
- At weddings and betrothals (cacao was part of the marriage contract)
- As offerings to the gods, particularly [Kukulkan](/blog/kukulkan-feathered-serpent-god/) and the maize god
- By healers as medicine

When Hernán Cortés arrived in Mexico in 1519 and encountered the Aztec emperor Moctezuma, who reportedly drank 50 cups of cacao per day, the Europeans were baffled. They brought the cacao back to Spain, where it was sweetened, warmed, and transformed into the hot chocolate we know today.

The sweetened version spread across Europe within decades. The original Mayan version nearly disappeared.

For the ritual practice the Maya built around cacao, see our guide to the [Mayan cacao ceremony](/blog/cacao-ceremony/). And for a complete picture of what the ancient Maya ate and drank, see our guide to [Mayan civilization food](/blog/mayan-civilization-food/).

## Ingredients

This is the most accurate recreation of the original drink that's still enjoyable for modern palates. You can control the bitterness by adjusting the cacao amount and the sweetness by adjusting the honey.

**For 1 serving:**
- 2 tablespoons raw cacao powder (or finely ground cacao nibs)
- 1 cup water, or oat milk for creaminess
- 1 small dried chili (árbol or ancho), or ¼ tsp cayenne powder
- ½ teaspoon ground cinnamon
- Pinch of sea salt
- 1 teaspoon raw honey, to taste (the Maya used *Jimerito*, a local stingless bee honey)
- Optional: ¼ tsp vanilla extract, pinch of allspice

**Why these ingredients?**
The chili was standard in Mayan cacao drinks, adding heat and stimulant properties. Cinnamon was a common Mesoamerican spice (though originally it was likely Mexican cinnamon, also called *canela*, which is softer than Ceylon or Cassia). Sea salt amplifies chocolate flavor. The honey adds just enough sweetness to balance without masking the cacao's complexity.

## Instructions

### Step 1: Warm Your Liquid
Pour the water (or oat milk) into a small saucepan over medium heat. You want it very warm but not boiling, around 80°C / 175°F. Boiling destroys some of cacao's more delicate aromatic compounds.

### Step 2: Whisk in the Cacao
Add the cacao powder and whisk continuously until fully dissolved and smooth. No lumps.

### Step 3: Add the Spices
Add the chili (whole), cinnamon, and salt. If you're using cayenne powder instead of a whole chili, add it now. Reduce heat and simmer for 3 minutes, stirring occasionally.

### Step 4: Remove the Chili and Froth
Fish out the whole chili. Now comes the most important step: **create the froth.**

The Maya poured the drink from one vessel to another, from a height, to create foam. You can replicate this with a milk frother (the easiest method), or by whisking vigorously for 60–90 seconds, or by using a blender for 15 seconds.

The froth is not optional. It's the difference between a flat cacao drink and something genuinely special.

### Step 5: Sweeten and Serve
Pour into a cup. Add honey to taste. The Maya drank this from gourd bowls and clay cups, which cool faster than ceramic, so drink it while it's warm.

## Variations

**Cold version (traditional):** Let the drink cool, then pour over ice. The original Mayan drink was often served cold. The pouring from height created the froth even with cold liquid.

**Richer version:** Replace water with oat milk or a mix of oat milk and coconut milk for a creamier texture closer to modern hot chocolate.

**Modern spiced version:** Add a pinch of allspice and cardamom alongside the cinnamon. Not traditional but excellent.

**Double strength:** Use 3 tablespoons of cacao powder for a darker, more intense cup. This is closer to what Mayan elites would have drunk. They didn't dilute their cacao.

## Why Cacao Nibs Work Better Than Powder

If you want to take this recipe further, use finely ground cacao nibs instead of commercial cacao powder. The difference is significant.

Cacao nibs are simply roasted cacao beans broken into small pieces. When you grind them (in a spice grinder or high-speed blender), you get raw cacao in its closest-to-original form, with the full fat (cacao butter) intact. This creates a richer, silkier texture and a more complex flavor than processed cacao powder.

The Maya used freshly ground cacao paste. We can't quite replicate that without industrial equipment, but finely ground nibs come close.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is Mayan hot chocolate called?**
The ancient Mayan word for their cacao drink was *xocolātl* (also spelled xocolatl). This Nahuatl word is the origin of the English word "chocolate."

**Did the Maya drink hot or cold chocolate?**
Both, but cold was common. The Maya created froth by pouring the liquid from a height, and cold liquid holds froth well. Some versions were consumed warm, but the cold, frothy version appears frequently in Mayan art and texts.

**What chili did the Maya use in chocolate?**
Various peppers native to Mesoamerica, similar to modern árbol, ancho, or habanero varieties. The heat varied by region and preference. For this recipe, a mild árbol or ancho works well; add cayenne if you want more heat.

**Is Mayan hot chocolate bitter?**
More bitter than modern hot chocolate, yes, but this recipe is balanced with honey and spices to be genuinely pleasant. Pure cacao without sweetener is very bitter. Use enough honey to balance your taste.

**Can I use cocoa powder instead of cacao powder?**
Yes, but the flavor will be different. Raw cacao powder is less processed and retains more complex flavor compounds. Cocoa powder (Dutch-processed) has been alkalized, which reduces acidity and bitterness but also removes some flavor depth. Both work in this recipe.

**What did the Maya use instead of sugar?**
Honey. The Maya kept stingless bees (*Melipona beecheii*, called *Xunan Kab*) and harvested their honey for sweetening food and drink. Stingless bee honey has a more complex, slightly acidic flavor than conventional honey.
