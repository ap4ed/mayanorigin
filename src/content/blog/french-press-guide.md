## Why French Press Is Worth Learning

The French press is the most forgiving and most unforgiving brewer at the same time.

Forgiving because there's no paper filter stealing your coffee's natural oils and body. What comes out is exactly what went in: full-flavored, rich, and real. Unforgiving because every mistake, wrong grind, wrong temperature, wrong timing, shows up clearly in the cup with nowhere to hide.

Done right, French press produces the richest, most full-bodied coffee you can brew at home without expensive equipment. It's also the method most similar to how the Maya prepared their own ancient drinks: whole ingredients, water, time, and patience.

## What You Need

You don't need much. That's part of why French press has been a coffee staple for over a century.

- French press (any size: 2-cup, 4-cup, or 8-cup all work)
- Coarsely ground coffee (this is the most important variable)
- Water just off the boil (93–96°C / 200–205°F)
- Timer
- Kitchen scale (strongly recommended, eyeballing fails consistently)

Optional but helpful:
- A dedicated coffee grinder (burr grinder, not blade)
- A thermometer if your kettle doesn't have one
- A wooden or plastic stirring stick (metal scratches glass)

## The Right Ratio: Coffee to Water

The standard specialty coffee ratio for French press is **1:15 coffee to water by weight.**

That means for every 1 gram of coffee, you use 15 grams (milliliters) of water. Here's how that looks in practice:

| Press Size | Coffee | Water |
|---|---|---|
| 2-cup (350ml) | 23g | 350ml |
| 4-cup (600ml) | 40g | 600ml |
| 8-cup (1L) | 67g | 1,000ml |

If you don't have a scale, use roughly **1 heaping tablespoon per 100ml of water**, but a scale gives you consistency you can't get by eye.

Want a stronger cup? Go to 1:14. Weaker? 1:16. Adjust one variable at a time until you find what you like.

## Grind Size: The Most Important Variable

**Grind coarse.** This is where most people go wrong.

For French press, your grind should look like coarse sea salt or rough breadcrumbs. Not fine like espresso, not medium like drip. Genuinely coarse.

Why? Because French press steeps for 4 minutes with no filter. Fine grounds extract too fast and too much, producing bitter, astringent coffee. Coarse grounds extract at the right speed for a full immersion brew.

A good burr grinder makes this easy. A cheap blade grinder makes it difficult, since blade grinders chop inconsistently, creating a mix of fine and coarse particles that extract at different rates. For a full breakdown of every grind size and which brew method it suits, see our [coffee grind size guide](/blog/coffee-grind-size-guide/).

**Quick test:** After the plunge, if the plunger meets heavy resistance and is hard to push down, your grind is too fine. If it falls through easily with no resistance, your grind is too coarse. You want steady, moderate resistance over 20–30 seconds.

## Step-by-Step: Perfect French Press

### 1. Preheat your press
Pour boiling water into the empty press, swirl, and dump it out. This keeps your brew temperature stable so the first sip isn't lukewarm.

### 2. Grind and weigh your coffee
Grind coarse. Weigh your dose. Add coffee to the empty, dry press.

### 3. Start your timer and add all the water at once
Pour all the water in. Don't drizzle. Make sure every gram of coffee gets wet in the first pour.

### 4. Stir once, then cover
Stir gently once to break up any dry clumps. Place the lid on top with the plunger pulled all the way up. Don't plunge yet.

### 5. Steep for exactly 4 minutes
Set a timer. Don't wander off and forget. 4 minutes is the sweet spot for coarsely ground coffee: long enough to extract fully, short enough to avoid bitterness.

### 6. Plunge slowly
At 4 minutes, begin pressing the plunger down with steady, even pressure. It should take 20–30 seconds to reach the bottom. If you're forcing it, stop. Your grind is too fine and you're compacting grounds that will make your coffee bitter and muddy.

### 7. Pour immediately
Don't let the coffee sit in the press after plunging. The grounds are still in contact with the water and will continue extracting, turning your cup astringent. Pour everything out right away.

## Temperature: Don't Skip This

Water temperature matters more than most people realize.

Too hot (boiling at 100°C / 212°F) and you'll scorch the coffee and extract harsh compounds. Too cool (below 90°C / 194°F) and you'll under-extract: flat, weak, sour.

The ideal range is **93–96°C / 200–205°F.** If you're bringing water to a full boil, let it sit off the heat for 30–45 seconds before pouring. That's usually enough.

If you have a variable-temperature kettle, set it to 94°C and forget about it.

## Troubleshooting Common Problems

**Too bitter:** Grind is too fine, or steep time is too long. Go coarser and/or pull back to 3:30.

**Too weak or sour:** Grind is too coarse, or steep time is too short. Go finer and/or extend to 4:30.

**Muddy, gritty texture:** Grind is too fine and particles are getting through the mesh. Go coarser.

**Coffee got cold fast:** You didn't preheat the press. Warm it with boiling water first.

**Hard to plunge:** Grind is too fine. Go coarser and don't force it through.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the French press ratio?**
The standard ratio is 1:15 coffee to water by weight. For a typical 8-cup press, that's about 67g of coffee to 1,000ml of water.

**How long should I steep French press coffee?**
4 minutes is the standard steep time with coarsely ground coffee. Too long and you'll over-extract and get bitterness. Too short and the coffee will taste flat and weak.

**What grind size for French press?**
Coarse, like rough sea salt. This is the single most important variable for French press. Too fine a grind causes bitterness and a muddy texture.

**Can you use fine grind in a French press?**
Technically yes, but the results are usually poor: bitter, astringent, and gritty. Fine grind is designed for espresso. French press needs coarse.

**Why is my French press coffee bitter?**
Usually one of two things: grind is too fine, or steep time is too long. Try a coarser grind first. That's the most common culprit.
